01 · Reading foundations

Reading gaze reflects an interaction between reader and text.

Reading is not a smooth sweep across every word. The eyes stop briefly, jump forward, skip predictable words, and sometimes return to earlier material. These events reveal processing, but their meaning depends on both the person and the passage.

Fixation

A pause near a word

The tracker records position and duration. Longer time can reflect lexical difficulty, importance, integration, distraction, or individual strategy.

Skip

No direct fixation on a word

Short, frequent, or predictable words are more likely to be processed parafoveally and skipped. A skip is not proof that the word was ignored.

Regression

A return to earlier text

Can indicate reanalysis, resolving a reference, checking evidence, or a task-driven search. Its direction and target matter.

Refixation

Another fixation on the same word

Often occurs for long or difficult words, but can also arise from landing-position error.

Common word-level time measures

1first-fixation durationduration of the first fixation on a word
2gaze durationsum before leaving the word for the first time
3total reading timeall fixations, including later returns
4go-past timetime until moving beyond the word to the right
5skip probabilitywhether the word received no direct fixation
6regression probabilitywhether gaze returned from or to the word
One duration does not map to one cognitive state.

The same 400 ms dwell can come from a rare word, a difficult sentence, a careful reader, a question-driven search, or a tracking error. leXic adds text context so the model does not need to infer all of these explanations from behavior alone.

02 · Lexical context

Three simple cues describe different sources of processing cost.

Word propertieslength · frequency · surprisal

Describe visual form, prior exposure, and contextual expectation.

→
Observed readingfixation · skip · regression

Combines text cost with ability, goal, and strategy.

→
Outcomequestion response

An imperfect behavioral reference for passage understanding.

Word length
The number of characters or subunits in a word. It approximates visual extent and often correlates with morphological complexity. It is easy to compute but language- and tokenization-dependent.
Word frequency
How often a word appears in a chosen reference corpus. Repeated exposure generally makes lexical access easier, but corpus choice, casing, inflection, and out-of-vocabulary handling must be reported.
Surprisal
I(wᵢ) = −log P(wᵢ | w₁…wᵢ₋₁). A less expected word receives higher surprisal. If the probability halves, surprisal rises by one bit when base-2 logs are used.
high probability in context→low surprisal·low probability in context→high surprisal

Frequency is context-independent: it asks whether a word is common in a corpus. Surprisal is context-dependent: “bank” can be common yet surprising in a particular sentence. Length captures a third, more surface-level source of cost. Their correlations make individual and combination ablations necessary.

Deployment claim

GPT-2 is used to compute surprisal before model deployment. The method is not language-model-free; the precise description is precomputed lexical conditioning with no language-model inference in the prediction loop.

03 · OneStop

A controlled reading dataset connects goals, text difficulty, gaze, and answers.

OneStop contains eye tracking from 360 participants reading news articles under ordinary reading and information-seeking regimes. The released data comprise about 152 hours of eye tracking, 2.6 million word tokens, and 486 comprehension questions. Text difficulty and repeated-reading manipulations make it possible to ask how reading behavior changes with both material and goal.

The target is not “true comprehension” in the abstract. It is whether a particular reader answered a particular question correctly after reading. Guessing, question quality, memory, and answer strategy all add label noise.

EyeBench

Generalization is defined by who and what are new.

EyeBench v1.0 standardizes seven predictive tasks across six reading datasets and currently presents 15 implemented models under one evaluation framework. leXic uses its OneStop reading-comprehension setup and split logic.

Unseen reader

New person

The text distribution is familiar, but the participant was absent from training. Tests transfer across ability, strategy, and gaze style.

Unseen text

New passage

The reader may be familiar, but the passage was absent. Tests whether the model generalizes beyond item-specific patterns.

Unseen both

New person + passage

Neither side of the interaction was observed. Usually the most demanding deployment-like shift.

Why a random row split is invalid.

If the same reader and passage appear on both sides, a model can exploit identity-specific patterns rather than the intended relationship between reading behavior and outcome. Reader and item grouping are part of the task definition.

Approach

Two ways to add lexical context to a gaze model.

LEXIC-Concat

Direct feature fusion

Align each fixation with its word, normalize word length, frequency, and precomputed GPT-2 surprisal using training statistics, then concatenate them to the gaze feature sequence.

Strength · simple, auditable, and stable
LEXIC-Res

Typical-reader residual

First estimate the gaze expected from lexical difficulty, then give the comprehension model the difference between expected and observed behavior.

Risk · “typical” gaze may be reader- and corpus-specific
Concat path

Let the predictor learn interactions

  • Observed gaze stays intact
  • Lexical features are available at every aligned step
  • The downstream model decides when text context matters
versus
Residual path

Explain expected behavior first

  • A lexical model predicts typical gaze
  • Residual emphasizes deviation from expectation
  • Error in the expectation model propagates downstream

Alignment checks before training

Token audit

Tracker word ↔ lexical token

Verify punctuation, contractions, subword aggregation, skipped words, repeated tokens, and unknown words.

Leakage audit

Normalization ↔ fold

Fit scalers, frequency defaults, residual models, and ensemble choices only on the training partition.

Missingness audit

No fixation ≠ no feature

Separate skipped words, off-text gaze, tracker loss, and failed alignment rather than assigning the same zero.

06 · Current evidence

A small predictive signal exists; its practical meaning remains open.

The current preprint reports ensembles of five seeds over ten folds. Both lexical variants improve unseen-text AUROC by approximately 1.8–2.2 percentage points, and Concat improves unseen-reader AUROC by about 2.9 points.

How to read AUROC

AUROC is the probability that a randomly selected correct-response example receives a higher score than a randomly selected incorrect-response example. A value of 0.5 is chance ranking and 1.0 is perfect ranking. AUROC does not select an operating threshold, measure calibration, or tell us how many errors occur in practice.

Observed
  • Small AUROC improvements under some shifts
  • Concat is the more stable design
  • Residual transfer is weaker
  • Evaluation uses matched folds and seed ensembles
Plausible interpretation
  • Lexical context explains part of gaze-only error
  • Simple fusion may be sufficient
  • A typical-reader gaze model may be population-specific
  • Text context may matter differently by shift
Not established
  • Practical decision value
  • Contribution of each feature
  • Transfer to another dataset or backbone
  • Calibration and subgroup reliability
  • Deployment-cost advantage
Effect size before novelty.

Known psycholinguistic features and direct concatenation are not an architectural breakthrough. The research value comes from a controlled account of where a small signal is repeatable, what causes it, and whether it is useful enough to justify its cost.

Revision program

Turn a small gain into an explanatory study.

The initial model and evaluation exist. The next question is whether the gain can be attributed, generalized, calibrated, and justified by total cost.

Gate 01Attribution

Which cue causes the gain?

Run length, frequency, surprisal, all three pairwise combinations, and all features with identical folds, seeds, normalization, and ensemble policy.

Advance if
Effects repeat and interactions explain more than one dominant cue.
Simplify if
One inexpensive feature retains nearly all of the gain.
Gate 02Robustness

Does the result survive another model and dataset?

Repeat on at least one different gaze backbone and a compatible corpus or EyeBench task. Freeze preprocessing before seeing the new test result.

Advance if
The direction remains stable under more than one shift.
Narrow if
The effect is specific to OneStop or AhnCNN.
Gate 03Decision quality

Are predictions calibrated and useful?

Add AUPRC, balanced accuracy, ECE, Brier score, confidence intervals, and reader/text subgroup errors. Compare thresholded decisions only after validation calibration.

Advance if
Ranking gains translate into calibrated improvements at a relevant operating point.
Reframe if
AUROC rises but actionable error does not.
Gate 04Total cost

Is the “inexpensive” context actually inexpensive?

Separate one-time surprisal precomputation from prediction-time parameters, latency, memory, storage, data alignment, and operations. Compare with a small online text encoder and simpler frequency-only variants.

Advance if
The chosen feature set has a clear accuracy–calibration–cost advantage.
Drop claim if
Offline computation and maintenance erase the deployment benefit.
Working research questionWhich precomputed lexical cues improve gaze-based comprehension prediction under reader and text shift, and are those gains attributable, calibrated, robust, and inexpensive enough to matter?
